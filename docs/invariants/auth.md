**Staff impersonation rules (IMP-1 through IMP-7, 2026-10-07 — NON-NEGOTIABLE).** A staff member can open a support session INSIDE a seller's or a reseller store's account and see exactly what they see. The seller is never told, because the session is not a thing they need to approve — it is a thing the system has to be able to prove afterwards, in full, to them or to anybody.

1. **IMP-1: three permissions, and the split between them is the whole oversight story.** `support.impersonate` opens a session; `support.impersonate.write` additionally lets it CHANGE things; `support.impersonate.review` reads every session anybody opened and ends one still running. **`support.impersonate.write` is RESERVED and declared on no route** — `@RequirePermissions(a, b)` means a OR b, so "always `support.impersonate`, and ALSO this one when the body asks for write" cannot be said in metadata at all; it is checked when the session is OPENED and then lives on the row as `mayWrite`, which the guard reads per request. A route gate would also be the wrong shape: the permission does not open one endpoint, it decides whether a hundred ordinary seller endpoints answer for half an hour. **All three are withheld from the Admin tier and from every seeded preset** (`ADMIN_EXCLUDES_IMPERSONATION`, the SIXTH bypass beside CLAUDE.md's five): the Admin preset is DERIVED from the catalogue, so without that entry a new key lands in it by construction — and an Admin holding `support.impersonate` AND `support.impersonate.review` reviews their own sessions, which makes the check that justifies the feature a check on nobody. **A two-person rule one role satisfies alone is not a rule.** They are granted deliberately, through `rbac.manage`, to named people on each side; a SUPER_ADMIN holds both implicitly and that is accepted (it is the owner).

2. **IMP-2: the code is a SECOND FACTOR, and the row exists before the code is cleared.** `start` creates the `impersonation_sessions` row and mails a six-digit code to the STAFF member's own address; the session is unusable until `otpVerifiedAt` is set. Creating the row at SEND time rather than at verify time is deliberate: **an abandoned attempt is still visible to a reviewer**, which is exactly the attempt worth seeing. The code dies in 10 minutes, the session in 30 (`IMPERSONATION_SESSION_TTL_MINUTES`) and **is never extended** — a support session that can be renewed is just a second login. Five wrong guesses and the attempt is over. **Two bugs here were the same bug twice:** the cap returned `ATTEMPTS_EXHAUSTED` while leaving the live code usable, and the exhaustion branch then called `revoke`, which deleted the very fail counter that had tripped it. `burnCode` (the code alone) and `revoke` (code AND counter) are separate for that reason — `impersonation_sessions` deliberately holds neither the code nor the attempt count, because those are ten-minute facts about one unfinished attempt and the row is the permanent record.

3. **IMP-3: the credential is a SIGNED `__Host-impersonation` cookie, and the cross-origin hop is a one-minute HANDOFF TOKEN.** `__Host-` cookies are bound to the origin that set them, so a cookie minted at `admin.` cannot be read at `app.` — which is why `verify` returns a handoff token (60 s, single use) that the seller or store app spends at its OWN origin for its own cookie. The session id is a uuidv7 and therefore not a secret; the HMAC is what makes holding the cookie mean something. **The exchange is TWO ROUTES, not one taking `kind: 'STORE'`**: which kind of account a session is for is decided when it is opened and must not become something the caller restates — `/auth/seller/impersonation/exchange` and `/auth/store/impersonation/exchange`, same service, binding from the route.

4. **IMP-4: what a session may never do is a DENY LIST, and the list is only safe because a SWEEP checks it.** `forbidden-while-impersonating.ts` names what is refused whatever permission the staff member holds: passwords, email, bank details, API keys, webhook endpoints, invitations, roles, terms, withdrawals, top-up claims, wallet payouts, and the acts that cannot be taken back in the physical world (turning a parcel round at the seller's cost, cancelling a consignment or a goods receipt — each has a STAFF-side equivalent, so refusing it here loses support nothing and keeps the record honest about who decided). The test is the file's own: **lasting access, or money that has moved.** **A deny list does NOT make a new route safe by default**, and that file's docblock claimed it did for a whole branch while naming as its enforcement a spec that checked a hand-written set of routes somebody had already thought about — so the claim held for exactly the routes it was not needed for. **Eleven live reseller-store routes were reachable the whole time**, four of them money (`.../wallet/payouts` is the seller's money leaving their wallet) and four of them standing access; `.../terms` sets the fee split and credit timing for every later order and was open while the store ACCEPTING those terms was refused, which is backwards. The enforcement is now the sweep in `impersonation-deny-list.spec.ts`: every mutating seller and store route, read out of the controllers, must be either on the deny list with the harm or on `DELIBERATELY_ALLOWED` with the reason it is safe. **Neither is the default, so the decision is forced in the commit that adds the route.** Three further rules, each learned by getting it wrong: **(a) a prefix that matches nothing refuses nothing, SILENTLY** — written from memory the list said `/seller/webhooks` and `/seller/withdrawals`, and the real routes are `webhook-endpoints` and `wallet/withdrawal-{requests,schedule}`; a test that feeds `refusalFor` its own prefixes passes perfectly against a prefix guarding nothing, so only a test that reads the controllers can catch it. **(a2) THE MATCH IS CASE-INSENSITIVE, and that is not a nicety — it is the list's entire safety.** Express routes case-BLIND by default (`case sensitive routing` is `undefined` and this app never sets it) while `req.path` carries whatever the caller typed; measured on the express 5.2.1 in this workspace, `POST /SELLER/api-keys` reaches the `/seller/api-keys` handler with a 200. A case-SENSITIVE matcher therefore did not weaken the deny list, it REMOVED it: every entry was walked past by capitalising one letter — an API key minted under the seller's name, a bank detail changed, a wallet paid out — and SILENTLY, because no refusal fired and so no `impersonation.request_refused` audit row was written either. The uppercasing of the METHOD beside a raw `path` was the asymmetry that should have been the tell. **The sweep structurally cannot catch this** (it feeds `refusalFor` the controllers' own canonical-case paths), so `forbidden-while-impersonating.spec.ts` feeds every entry back in three manglings. Fixed in the MATCHER, deliberately not by flipping Express to case-sensitive routing: a security list compared against a caller-controlled string must not depend on how another layer happens to be configured, and flipping the app would start 404ing working requests across the whole estate to fix one function. **Any future route-matching security check in this codebase is case-insensitive, or it is not a check.** **(b) OVER-matching is the safe direction and must stay** — `/seller/api-keys` also refuses `/seller/api-keys-something`, deliberately; tightening the tail to a segment boundary looks like a correctness fix, was tried, and silently reopened `PATCH /seller/profile/bank-details` and both `password-reset` routes, which had only ever been covered by half-segment matching. If a real route needs to live below a forbidden prefix and be writable, the fix is a more precise entry, never a looser matcher. Those three are named in full now anyway, because a prefix depending on half a segment breaks the next time somebody has the same good idea. **(c) `:param` segments and `exact`** exist because a collection can be dangerous while its members are not: `POST /seller/reseller-stores` mails an invitation, `PUT /seller/reseller-stores/:id/catalogue/:variantId` is a price edit, and as a plain prefix the first swallowed the second.

5. **IMP-5: the audit row carries BOTH identities, through `AsyncLocalStorage`, because ~200 call sites cannot be asked to remember.** `impersonation-als.middleware.ts` puts the live session in ambient context and `AuditLogService` is the ONE reader: it upgrades `actorType` to `STAFF_AS_SELLER` / `STAFF_AS_STORE` and stamps `audit_logs.impersonated_by_staff_user_id`, while **`actorId` stays the seller or store user** — the act really was performed as them, and rewriting that would make every existing per-seller audit query miss it. **The session's OWN lifecycle routes open NO context** (`SESSION_LIFECYCLE_PATHS` in the ALS middleware, matched case-insensitively for the reason above): entering and leaving are acts ON the session, not acts inside the account. `exchange` states the rule — "the honest row is 'this person entered'" — and with the middleware running on `*` the symmetric row came out as `STAFF_AS_SELLER`, so pressing End on the banner recorded **"the seller ended the support session"**. A reviewer asking what a staff member did missed the session-end; one reading the seller's activity saw one the seller never performed; and `audit_logs` is append-only, so neither is correctable. Nothing on those routes needs the context — each reads the signed cookie itself, clears it, or creates the session. `requestCount` / `writeCount` on the session are a SORT KEY for the review screen, not the record; the record is `audit_logs`, written synchronously per request.

6. **IMP-6: the way out ENDS the session, and the bar saying so cannot be dismissed.** The banner renders off `identity.impersonation`, which exists only on a session a staff member opened — so there is no flag to get backwards and no way for a real seller to see it. It is loud on purpose (the account's name in full, a danger fill, a viewport edge no scroll position hides, the time left counting down) because the risk this feature carries is one person who has forgotten whose account they are in. **There is no close control**: the bar is a CONDITION, not an event, and a condition that can be clicked away will be, in the first minute, by the person most likely to need it in the twentieth. Its button calls `POST /auth/{seller,store}/impersonation/end`, which writes the end row using the cookie as the credential, and falls back to `/leave` only when there is no live session left to end — `leave` drops the cookie and leaves the session RUNNING, which is the wrong default by a distance: a session that reads as live while nobody is inside it is a false record in the one place this feature exists to keep honest. A live-session count also sits in the ADMIN header on every page, because the failure mode is nobody looking at the review screen.

7. **IMP-7b: the per-request counter is a FIRE-AND-FORGET DB WRITER and is drained like every other one.** `ImpersonationGuard` fires `noteRequest` with `void` on purpose — the seller's request must not wait on a sort key — and `ImpersonationService` therefore holds its own in-flight Set with a public `drainInFlight()`, awaited by `resetAuthState`. Undrained, its `UPDATE impersonation_sessions` outlives the request holding FK RowShareLocks on `staff_users`, `sellers` and `seller_stores` (all RESTRICT) while the harness deletes them: the `40P01` that names neither the test nor the cause. It is the ninth writer of this shape and NOTIF-19 already prescribed the remedy — "a new one belongs in the same list in the same commit that adds the writer".

7. **IMP-7: `ImpersonationRuntimeModule` must be imported by every module whose services inject from it.** It is not `@Global()`, so `AppModule` importing it means nothing to `SellerAuthModule`. Injecting `ImpersonationBannerService` into the seller and store auth services without adding that import compiled, typechecked and passed the whole unit suite — unit tests construct their subjects by hand and never resolve a dependency through DI — and then **every e2e spec in the repository failed identically**, with the reason swallowed by `logger: false`. `app-module-boots.spec.ts` is the gate for this; a red boot test presents as a hung or dead run, so search the output for `UnknownDependenciesException` rather than assuming the suite is stuck.

Honor IMP-1 through IMP-7 (staff impersonation, 2026-10-07). **Every route-matching security check is CASE-INSENSITIVE** — Express routes case-blind by default, so `POST /SELLER/api-keys` reached the handler while a case-sensitive deny list let it through, silently and with no refusal audit row. 2. **RBAC-1 (R0, REWRITTEN 2026-09-27 — the paragraph that stood here described a mechanism that had been DELETED, and a live hole rode in on its leftovers): the seller guard fails closed on READS AND WRITES, on PERMISSIONS.** `SellerJwtGuard` asks two questions and keeps no precedence table: `@SellerSelfService()` short-circuits the whole gate for an endpoint about the caller themselves (their session, their password, their own inbox — the token has already answered what a permission would ask); otherwise the handler's `@RequireSellerPermissions(...)`, else the class's, and holding ANY listed key passes. An OWNER role (`seller_roles.is_owner`) holds the whole catalogue implicitly, so a key added next release reaches it with no backfill. **An endpoint that declares NEITHER is refused** — `ENDPOINT_NOT_AUTHORIZED`, reads included — so forgetting an annotation makes a surface unreachable rather than open. A refused request audits LOW `seller.access_denied_permission` and 403s `INSUFFICIENT_PERMISSION`. **The cosmetic half lives in `apps/seller/src/lib/role-access.ts`** — ONE table read by both the nav filter and the route boundary, so a link can never point at a page that refuses to render.

   **What it replaced, and the bill for not finishing the replacement.** It was six fixed roles where five saw EVERYTHING and differed only in what they could CHANGE, with VIEWER's reads as an allow-list a controller opted into (`@SellerViewerReadable`) and writes gated by `@SellerRoles`. Commit `961726ea` moved to permissions and **deleted `customer-lookup`'s handler-level `@SellerRoles(OWNER, ADMIN, OPS)` with no replacement**, so that endpoint fell through to the class's `orders.view` — the ONE key the `viewer` role holds — and for weeks every seller login on the platform could pull PLATFORM-WIDE order counts, delivery and return rates for any phone number typed at it. Four things made that invisible, and each is a lesson worth more than the fix: the 16-line comment explaining why VIEWER must not reach it SURVIVED the decorator it described; the spec pinning it asserted `src.slice(at, at + 700)).toContain('@SellerRoles')` and the only occurrence left in the file was inside that comment, so it passed while testing nothing; the two decorator files stayed in the tree with long docblocks describing enforcement the guard no longer performed; and THIS paragraph described the deleted model and named that spec as the enforcement point. Five mutually consistent documents, all wrong, all agreeing. **A deleted mechanism must lose its scaffolding, its comments, its spec and its CLAUDE.md paragraph in the same change** — `@SellerRoles`, `@SellerViewerReadable` and `requireSellerRoles` are now deleted, and `seller-read-surface.spec.ts` asserts on the PERMISSION each GET resolves to (with comments STRIPPED first, because prose about a rule reads exactly like the rule to a regex). `customer-lookup` is behind `orders.create`, which reproduces owner / admin / ops exactly and names the act it is for. **Named enforcement points: `seller-jwt.guard.spec.ts` (the mechanism), `seller-permission-surface.spec.ts` (every endpoint declares something, and no WRITE rests on a `.view` key), `seller-read-surface.spec.ts` (WHICH key each GET ends up behind, by name — a new GET on a broadly-gated controller joins that key's surface by inheritance and nobody decides), and `controllers-declare-a-guard.spec.ts` (a controller with NO `@UseGuards` is invisible to all three permission-surface specs, because each bails on its own guard name — so it is both unauthenticated and unasserted).**

   **AMENDED 2026-09-30 — there is a SECOND door, and it neither scoped nor authorised.** `ApiKeyGuard` (`Authorization: Bearer skd_…`) admits a seller with no person behind it, and it did two things wrong that only mattered together. It granted every key `ALL_SELLER_PERMISSION_KEYS` minus `roles.manage` — the built-in Admin role, handed to a credential a seller pastes into third-party software, able to request a withdrawal, change the bank account the withdrawal is paid into, invite a colleague and mint more keys. And it **returned true the moment the key checked out**, consulting no `@RequireSellerPermissions` at all, so the fail-closed rule above held for a browser session and was simply absent for a key. Nothing was exposed and nothing failed, because the guard is wired to NO controller — `@UseGuards(ApiKeyGuard)` appears nowhere, so the keys the seller UI mints authenticate nothing. **That is why it could sit there, and why it is fixed before the first controller is attached: a key that has been issued cannot be un-issued.** Now: `seller_api_keys.scopes` (TEXT[], empty default) is chosen at create and `common/auth/seller-api-key-scopes.ts` is the ONE place a scope becomes permissions — the `BinPolicyService` / `WarehouseResolverService` discipline, because a second place deciding what `orders:read` means is how the two come to disagree. **The ceiling is the ABSENCE of a scope**: there is none covering the wallet, withdrawals, the company profile and its bank account, the team, roles, other keys, webhooks, charges, freight or reseller stores, so money and identity are unreachable by a machine key by construction rather than by an operator not ticking a box. An unrecognised scope name is DROPPED, never widened; a key whose scopes resolve to nothing is refused by name (`API_KEY_NO_SCOPES` — "mint a new one") rather than left to 403 on each endpoint in turn. And the RBAC decision itself now lives in the pure `sellerAuthorizationVerdict` (`common/auth/seller-authorization.ts`), which BOTH guards read — two doors into the same rooms must not be able to disagree about who may come in; each still audits and throws in its own words, because a refused person and a refused machine are different facts. Pinned by `seller-api-key-scopes.spec.ts`, which names the forbidden permissions one by one and **scans the guard's source, comments stripped, for `ALL_SELLER_PERMISSION_KEYS`** — restoring the blanket grant reads as a one-line simplification, and stripping the comments first is the lesson from the `@SellerRoles` spec that asserted on a docblock and passed while testing nothing.

   **AMENDED 2026-10-05 (RBAC-1b): a person holds SEVERAL roles, and their permissions are the UNION.** `{staff,seller_user,store_user}_roles` join tables replaced the single `role_id` FK on all three identities, and every guard resolves the union of every live role. **The super-powers are ANY-semantics**: one role with `isSuperAdmin` (staff) or `isOwner` (seller/store) grants the whole catalogue whatever else is held. That is not a detail — a superuser role carries NO permission rows, its power is the flag, so a naive union of rows would have silently DEMOTED anybody holding Super admin beside a narrow role, and a demotion looks exactly like a working feature until somebody cannot do something. Pinned in both list orders. **Zero live roles grants nothing and is refused**, asserted even on a self-service route; a soft-deleted role drops out of the union, including a soft-deleted superuser role. **Staff gained three access tiers beside the seven job functions** — Admin (74 of 84: not who-has-access, not the company's financial identity, not the five SUPER_ADMIN-by-construction bypasses), Support, and Read-only (every `.view` key, derived not listed). Job functions stayed because a picker and a finance person need DIFFERENT permissions, not fewer, and multiple roles is what lets the two axes compose instead of needing a bespoke role per person.

   **`role_id` and four legacy enum columns still exist and decide NOTHING.** The join table is the only authority; the columns are transitional labels, and a FOLLOW-UP migration drops them. That is expand/contract and here it is forced rather than tidy: `scripts/deploy.sh` runs `prisma migrate deploy` at line 147 and builds the apps at 158, so the schema changes MINUTES before the new code runs and anything the old code still reads must survive that window. **`staff_users.role`, `seller_users.role` and the two invitation enums are now NULLABLE** — the three new tiers have no enum spelling, so a Support-only invitee has nothing honest to put there; "none" is the ordinary case, not an edge. Two queries were already reading that enum to pick PEOPLE (`admin-agent.service.ts` for the call roster, `invite-lead.service.ts` for who gets told) and both now ask a permission through the join table; the second is the dangerous shape, because it chooses an audience and so it does not fail, it quietly tells nobody. **Before writing `where: { role: … }` against a legacy enum, stop — the answer is a permission predicate through the join table.**

   **Invitations carry roleS.** `CreateStaffInvitationDto` validated `@IsEnum(StaffRole)`, so **nobody could be invited onto a role their own team had created** — the only route was to invite onto one of the legacy seven and change it afterwards. The seller DTO had the identical bug. Both take `roleIds` now; the STORE takes `roleKeys` and stays key-based, deliberately, because nothing anywhere creates a custom store role (`provisionDefaultStoreRoles` is the only writer), so `@IsIn(STORE_ROLE_KEYS)` is the complete vocabulary and costs nothing — **if store role creation ever lands, that binding becomes exactly the staff bug.** Assignment is `PATCH .../roles` and REPLACES the set.

   **The seller side gained a `member` preset (2026-10-07).** Six fixed presets became seven: "runs the business day to day, including the reseller channel — everything except changing who can get in, and everything except moving money out". Two things it deliberately does NOT grant, each because the thing asked for does not exist in the shape it was asked for: **`profile.manage`** is withheld while `profile.view` is granted, because the company profile carries the BANK ACCOUNT and that decides where the money lands; and the per-PINCODE rate card the brief named is not grantable at all — pricing has been a FLAT fee since the zone/slab engine priced a production order at ₹0.00 (PRC-1..8), so the preset grants `charges.view`, the breakdown that exists, rather than implying a screen that does not. Both are written down beside the keys, because "why can't a Member do X" is asked later and a silent omission reads as an oversight.

   **A gate that reads a SINGULAR role field is now wrong by construction**, and this bit: the store portal's owner gate was `me?.roleKey === 'owner'`, and `roleKey` is the FIRST grant — so somebody who was Finance AND Owner read as `finance` and had the owner-only controls hidden from them. Ask the question the server asks (`some(r => r.isOwner)`), never "is the first one".
